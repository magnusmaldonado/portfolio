Process Standardization

Email Intake & Workflow Standardization

Turning an informal email request process into a structured workflow with clear requirements, predictable turnaround times, and enough visibility to eliminate a recurring coordination meeting.

An urgent request isn't necessarily ready work.

I could stop everything to build an email someone needed “ASAP.” Only to discover that the campaign was still waiting on something that prevented it from being sent at all.

The problem wasn't that people occasionally needed something quickly. There was no reliable distinction between an actual emergency and work that simply entered the process incomplete.

Role

Marketing Automation Specialist

Environment

Marketing Automation · Campaign Operations · Project Management · Cross-Functional Marketing

Systems

Email requests were expected seven days in advance, but the expectation was informal. Incomplete tickets, placeholders, and last‑minute requests regularly disrupted planned work.

The Challenge

We had a seven‑day expectation. We didn't have a seven‑day process.

We told marketing stakeholders that email requests should be provided at least one week before the desired send date.

But there was no formal operating procedure defining what had to accompany that request.

A ticket could exist while pieces of the campaign were still being assembled.

Someone might have the email copy but not the header image. They might have the creative but still be waiting for a landing page. Tracking information, audience requirements, contact lists, or other dependencies could still be missing.

Placeholder requests could also be created before the information needed to complete the work actually existed.

A ticket existing in the system did not mean the work was ready to be done.

What Was Missing

  • Email content
  • Header or creative assets
  • Landing-page URLs
  • Tracking information
  • Audience requirements
  • Required approvals

The Workaround

The weekly meeting had become a manual completeness check.

Every week, marketing stakeholders were invited to a 30‑minute email‑calendar meeting.

Before that meeting, I reviewed the campaigns scheduled for the current week and the following week.

I went through the corresponding tickets and identified anything that was still missing.

Then I brought those questions to the meeting.

Do we have the landing page yet?

Is the creative ready?

Are we still waiting on another team for this information?

The meeting gave us one shared window to resolve those questions instead of chasing every stakeholder individually throughout the week.

It was useful for the process we had.

But it also meant I had to manually inspect upcoming work just to determine whether it was actually ready.

Before Every Meeting

  • Review current‑week tickets
  • Review next‑week tickets
  • Identify missing information
  • Determine who owned each dependency
  • Prepare stakeholder questions
  • Resolve gaps during the meeting
The meeting was compensating for information the intake process could not reliably collect on its own.

The Daily Impact

“We need this ASAP” could change my entire week.

I planned my workload ahead of time.

At the beginning of the week, I reviewed the campaigns assigned to me and organized my days around what needed to be built, reviewed, approved, and sent.

That plan could change quickly when unexpected requests arrived.

A day that had intentionally been kept lighter could suddenly contain multiple new campaigns from different stakeholders, all needing immediate attention.

The work I had already planned didn't disappear.

The urgent work was simply added on top of it.

What This Created

  • Constant reprioritization
  • Unpredictable daily workload
  • Interruptions to planned campaigns
  • Rush execution
  • Difficulty protecting existing commitments

The Readiness Problem

Sometimes the “urgent” email couldn't even be sent.

Some of the clearest examples came from promotional campaigns involving an event, incentive, gift, or other offer.

A stakeholder could urgently need the campaign built, so I would stop what I was doing and prioritize the email.

Then we would discover that the underlying promotion was still waiting for required legal or compliance approval.

That approval could take far longer than building the email itself.

The execution work had been rushed before a critical upstream dependency was complete.

The Bigger Impact

Last‑minute work didn't only affect my workload.

Multiple marketing teams communicated with overlapping audiences.

That meant the shared email calendar had limited capacity.

A stakeholder could request a last‑minute campaign only to discover that other campaigns were already scheduled for the same audience.

At that point, the question was no longer simply whether I could build the email quickly enough.

We also had to determine where it could actually fit without disrupting work another team had already planned.

The lack of standardized intake had become a capacity‑management problem.

Shared‑System Impact

  • Overlapping audiences
  • Limited send capacity
  • Last‑minute calendar conflicts
  • Properly planned campaigns could be disrupted

The Standard

Define what “ready” actually means.

The solution began by defining the information I actually needed before execution could begin.

My manager handled the Asana implementation of the request form, while I determined the operational requirements the workflow needed to capture based on what I needed to successfully build and schedule campaigns.

The process also distinguished between single emails and larger email series so that multi-touch campaigns could be planned appropriately.

And placeholder requests without the information needed for execution were no longer treated as ready work.

Definition of Ready

  • Complete content
  • Creative and header assets
  • Landing‑page URLs
  • Tracking information
  • Audience or list requirements
  • Desired send date
  • Cadence when applicable
  • Required approval status
If work was going to enter the execution queue, the information needed to execute it had to come with it.

Turnaround Expectations

Seven calendar days became an actual operating standard.

A complete email request needed to be submitted at least seven calendar days before the requested send date.

If a complete request was submitted on Tuesday, the earliest guaranteed send date was the following Tuesday.

That did not mean every campaign automatically waited seven days.

If my workload allowed me to complete something sooner, I could contact the stakeholder and offer an earlier send date.

Complete Request Tuesday
→
Earliest Guaranteed Send Next Tuesday

Earlier became something capacity allowed. Not something urgency demanded.

Making It Stick

A process people can bypass isn't really a process.

Initially, writing the requirements into an Asana request form was not enough.

In the past, when Sherry or I pushed back on incomplete or last‑minute work, stakeholders could escalate the request through management and the work would ultimately be pushed through anyway.

So during the beginning of the new process, my manager became the validation point.

Requests reached her first. She reviewed whether the required information had been provided.

If the request was incomplete, it did not move forward to me.

Once stakeholders became accustomed to the new standard, I could take over that validation myself.

Initial Workflow

  • Stakeholder submits request
  • Manager validates completeness
  • Incomplete work is returned
  • Complete work moves to execution
  • Validation later transitions to me
The requirements stopped being preferences from the people executing the work. They became the operating process.

Connected Improvements

The systems started working together.

Originally, stakeholders provided the Salesforce campaign information I needed so I could construct the appropriate tracking links while building their email.

Later, after I built the self‑service Campaign Tracking tool, stakeholders could create those structured tracking URLs themselves.

Instead of handing me the campaign information and waiting for me to build the URLs, they could include the completed tracking links with their request.

One system improvement removed work from another system.

Workflow Evolution

  • Previously: campaign information sent to me
  • I manually built tracking links
  • Later: stakeholders used the tracking builder
  • Completed URLs arrived with the request

Flexibility

Exceptions became manageable.

People still forgot things occasionally.

Someone might ask if I could get something out sooner than the standard turnaround.

Sometimes I could.

If the calendar had capacity and my workload allowed it, I was happy to help.

Other times I had to say no or offer the earliest date that was realistically available.

The difference was that last‑minute work was no longer the default.

With a Reliable Baseline

  • Actual capacity was visible
  • Exceptions could be evaluated individually
  • Planned work remained protected
  • Flexibility was still possible
A good process doesn't eliminate flexibility. It makes flexibility sustainable.

The Operational Payoff

The weekly meeting eventually became unnecessary.

Before standardization, the email‑calendar meeting gave us a dedicated place to resolve missing information.

But once the new intake process was established, I noticed that I wasn't coming to the meeting with questions anymore.

Campaigns were being submitted in advance.

The required information was already in the tickets.

The shared calendar showed what was scheduled.

I no longer needed to inspect upcoming work, make a list of missing information, and use 30 minutes of everyone's time to collect it.

So I questioned why we were still holding the meeting.

We stopped having it and gave everyone that time back.

The Bigger Lesson

Standardization doesn't mean removing human judgment.

Before the redesign, flexibility effectively meant that almost any request could become urgent.

Afterward, the default path became predictable:

Submit complete information → validate the request → schedule the campaign → execute the work.

That structure did not prevent me from helping someone when something genuinely unexpected happened.

It gave me enough visibility to decide when an exception was actually possible.

Systems Lesson

  • Define what “ready” means
  • Protect execution from incomplete work
  • Make turnaround expectations visible
  • Use leadership to reinforce new boundaries
  • Preserve flexibility for genuine exceptions
The goal of standardization wasn't to remove human judgment. It was to stop every request from requiring it.

The Challenge

Creating a tracking link only took a minute. Getting one could take hours.

Tracking links were a small but necessary part of nearly every campaign. Emails needed them. LinkedIn and other paid campaigns needed them. Landing pages and other digital marketing activities depended on them for attribution.

Before the builder, those URLs were created manually.

That meant typing out a series of query parameters and inserting the appropriate campaign information each time. The structure was repetitive, but it still left room for small mistakes: a parameter could be misspelled, a value could be entered incorrectly, or required tracking information could be left out.

And those small mistakes mattered.

If a tracking link was wrong, the problem wasn't simply a broken URL. Leads could be attributed incorrectly, campaign data could become unreliable, and troubleshooting became more complicated because the tracking link itself had to be investigated as a possible source of the problem.

The Problem

  • Tracking URLs were assembled manually
  • Small parameter errors could affect attribution
  • Campaign owners depended on someone with tracking knowledge
  • A one-minute task could create hours of waiting
  • The bottleneck was dependency, not task duration
The people who needed the links often weren't the people who knew how to build them.

As part of my marketing automation work, creating and testing tracking links became one of my responsibilities. Someone working on a campaign might reach the end of their task and then message me because they needed a tracking URL before they could finish.

The actual request might take me a minute.

But if I was working on something more urgent, their one‑minute request had to wait.

The bottleneck wasn't how long the task took. It was who the task depended on.

How I Found the Problem

It Started With My Own Shortcut

Long before I built the URL builder, I had already created a small workaround for myself.

Because tracking links followed predictable patterns, I kept reusable pieces of the structure in my own working documentation. Instead of recreating every URL from scratch, I reused the pieces that didn't change and updated only the campaign‑specific information.

It wasn't sophisticated. It was simply faster and reduced the number of things I had to remember or retype.

Then I watched someone else build a tracking link.

They typed the entire thing manually.

Every parameter. Every value. Every time.

What I Noticed

  • The tracking structure was predictable
  • I had already created a shortcut for myself
  • Other people were still rebuilding URLs manually
  • Paid campaigns sometimes had to wait for tracking links
  • The personal shortcut could become a shared tool
If the structure is predictable, why are we rebuilding it from scratch every single time?

That was when I realized the process I had optimized for myself wasn't how everyone else was working.

The issue became even more obvious with paid campaigns. A campaign could be ready to launch, but the person completing the work still needed someone else to create the tracking link.

What looked like a tiny repetitive task was actually creating unnecessary handoffs, last‑minute requests, inconsistent execution, and avoidable opportunities for error.

So instead of making my personal shortcut better, I built something everyone could use.

What I Built

A Simple Tool for a Repetitive Problem

I built a lightweight web‑ based URL builder using HTML, CSS, and JavaScript.

The goal wasn't to create another complicated platform.

It was to take a process that followed known rules and turn those rules into a tool.

Users entered the information specific to their campaign, and the builder assembled the standardized tracking URL automatically.

Built‑In Guidance

Self‑Service Had to Actually Mean Self‑Service

I didn't want the tool to require the same technical knowledge that the manual process required.

If users had to come ask me what every field meant, I would have simply moved the bottleneck.

So I added contextual guidance directly into the builder. Information icons provided examples and explanations for fields that might not be obvious, including what type of value the user needed to enter.

The goal was for someone to be able to open the builder and understand what to do without needing me standing beside them.

Designed for Self‑Service

  • Field guidance built directly into the tool
  • Examples explained what information belonged where
  • Users did not need technical tracking knowledge
  • The tool could be used without asking me for instructions
A self‑service tool isn't really self‑service if someone still has to explain how to use it every time.

The Workflow

Generate. Copy. Test. Verify.

Submitting the form generated the completed tracking URL.

From there, users could copy the finished URL directly to their clipboard and use the Test Link option to begin validation.

But generating the URL wasn't the end of the process.

I trained users to test their links in an incognito browser session before launch.

The builder could standardize the structure, but campaign‑specific information was still entered by a person. Human error was still possible.

Testing verified that the link:

  • Opened the correct destination
  • Carried the expected tracking information
  • Routed the lead into the appropriate campaign
  • Behaved as expected before real campaign traffic reached it

Automation removed unnecessary manual work. Testing protected the system from the manual work that remained.

Protecting the Data

The Tool Had to Fit the Rest of the System

Testing introduced another consideration: test leads.

Submitting forms during testing created records inside Salesforce and Eloqua. If those records weren't handled correctly, they could remain in the database, inflate lead counts, receive marketing emails, and affect reporting.

There was already an established safeguard within the existing environment for removing test records identified with a specific naming convention.

When I trained users on the new workflow, I incorporated that existing process into the instructions.

Users were taught how to create identifiable test records, verify their routing, and follow the established cleanup convention so forgotten test records wouldn't remain in production data.

The Full Workflow Included

  • Generating the tracking URL
  • Testing in an incognito session
  • Verifying lead routing
  • Using identifiable test records
  • Following the existing cleanup process
I wasn't just handing someone a URL generator. I was handing off the process surrounding it.

The Bigger Lesson

Sometimes the Best Automation Isn't Complicated

This project didn't require a new enterprise platform or an expensive piece of software.

The organization already had the systems it needed.

The problem existed between those systems.

People needed a reliable way to translate campaign information into a standardized tracking URL, test it correctly, and move on with their work without depending on someone else.

So I built the smallest solution that solved that problem.

Systems Lesson

  • Not every automation requires a complex platform
  • Small recurring dependencies can create large delays
  • Predictable rules are good candidates for automation
  • Self-service removes unnecessary handoffs
  • The smallest useful solution can be the right solution
The goal wasn't to automate everything. It was to remove the friction that didn't need to exist.

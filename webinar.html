Operations & Process Design

Webinar Process Redesign

Redesigning a high-volume webinar process to reduce unnecessary work, protect shared marketing capacity, and create a more sustainable way to plan and promote recurring events.

They created roughly 50 webinar projects at the beginning of the year to get ahead.

The problem was that every project created work for six or seven other people before most of that work was ready to be done.

What looked efficient at the beginning of the process was creating unnecessary administrative work downstream.

Role

Marketing Automation Specialist

Environment

Marketing Automation · Webinar Operations · Web Operations · Project Management

Systems

Asana · Salesforce · Email Marketing · Webinar Platform

The Challenge

Getting ahead was actually creating more work.

At the beginning of the year, roughly 50 Best Practice Webinar projects were created in Asana.

The intention made sense.

Rather than waiting for every webinar to approach, the team could create the year’s projects at once and everyone involved would know what was coming.

But the projects weren’t being created through a standardized intake process.

An existing webinar project was duplicated again and again.

The information that was immediately relevant to the webinar might be changed, such as the speaker and, when available, the title. But the project also contained multiple child tasks assigned to everyone responsible for executing the webinar.

Those tasks were duplicated too.

For me, a single webinar could contain several separate tasks representing different pieces of work I would eventually need to complete. Other people involved in the process had their own assigned tasks within that same project.

So when approximately 50 projects were created, it didn’t simply create 50 pieces of work.

It multiplied those projects across the task lists of every person involved in executing them.

Challenge Highlights

  • ~50 projects duplicated at the start of the year
  • Child tasks assigned before information was ready
  • Multiple people had to manually update their own task dates
  • Changing webinar plans created repeated downstream rework
  • No formal BPW SOP existed
The process looked efficient at the point where the work was created. The inefficiency only became visible when you followed that work downstream.

What Was Happening

Work Was Being Created Before It Was Ready

Many of those assignments couldn’t actually be completed yet.

The information didn’t exist.

But because the tasks already existed, each person still had to manage them.

Dates had to be reviewed and adjusted manually across individual child tasks. As webinar plans changed, those tasks had to be revisited again.

And the farther into the year we got, the less reliable the original plan became.

That wasn’t necessarily because anyone had done something wrong.

It was partly a consequence of how far ahead the webinar schedule was being created.

What This Created

  • Premature assignments
  • Manual date maintenance
  • Repeated task revisits
  • Work queues full of tasks that were not actionable yet

The Planning Problem

A commitment made in November isn’t necessarily a reliable plan for next September.

Near the end of the year, prospective speakers were given the opportunity to select dates for webinars throughout the following year.

Early in the year, that worked reasonably well.

Those commitments were still relatively recent. Speakers generally knew their schedules and the webinars moved forward.

As the year progressed, cancellations became more common.

Someone who had selected a date many months earlier might later discover a scheduling conflict, need to take vacation, or simply no longer be available.

Now the webinar had to move.

And because the work had already been created months earlier, changing the webinar didn’t affect one calendar entry.

It affected the downstream tasks already assigned to everyone involved.

Why It Became Unstable

  • Speakers committed months too far in advance
  • Schedule certainty decreased over time
  • Cancellations triggered work changes across multiple people
The farther the plan moved from the moment it was created, the less reliable it became — but the work had already been distributed as if the plan were final.

Operational Reality

There Was No Standard BPW Intake Process

Most marketing requests operated within established processes and lead times.

Best Practice Webinars did not have the same standardized operating procedure.

The program had existed for a long time, was considered a high priority, and its work was often accommodated whenever the required information became available.

That meant webinar information could arrive late and still become urgent work for the teams responsible for execution.

For each webinar, my responsibilities included building the promotional email, building the webinar reminder, preparing the post-webinar follow-up, maintaining the upcoming webinar landing page, and later creating the page where the recorded webinar and presentation materials would remain available.

After the webinar, I also had to process the registration information, prepare the webinar resources, update the appropriate pages, send the follow-up communication, and process the contacts who requested additional information.

Then the process began again for the next webinar.

With webinars intended to happen every Wednesday, inefficient planning didn’t create an occasional inconvenience.

It created a recurring operational problem.

Recurring Execution Work

  • Promotional email
  • Tuesday reminder email
  • Upcoming webinar landing page
  • Past webinar page with recording + PDF
  • Registrant and hand-raiser processing
  • Salesforce campaign updates

The Bigger Impact

The Problem Was Bigger Than BPW

The consequences didn’t stay inside the webinar program.

Best Practice Webinar communications were being sent to the same broad English-speaking audience used by other marketing campaigns.

To protect that audience from excessive communication, there was a limit on how frequently they could be emailed.

That meant the marketing calendar had finite capacity.

Other teams could follow the established process, provide everything on time, and have their campaigns ready to go.

Then a late BPW request could arrive.

Because BPW was considered high priority, accommodating that request could mean shifting work that had been properly planned.

Shared-System Impact

  • Same audience used by multiple campaigns
  • Limited email capacity
  • Late BPW requests competed with compliant campaigns
  • Teams following the process could still be displaced
A predictable process was repeatedly becoming an emergency — and other teams could pay the price even when they had followed the process correctly.

The Registration Problem

Late Promotion Created Another Problem

Simply squeezing a webinar email into the calendar wasn’t always enough.

Timing mattered.

The initial promotional email introduced the webinar, but the reminder sent the Tuesday before the Wednesday event often generated the largest influx of registrations.

When the initial promotion went out too late, there wasn’t enough time between the invitation, reminder, and webinar.

That reduced the amount of time available for registrations to accumulate.

And if registrations were too low, the webinar could be cancelled and rescheduled.

Which created another cycle of changes.

Late information
↓
Compressed promotion
↓
Less time to register
↓
Low registration risk
↓
Cancellation or rescheduling
↓
Downstream work changes again

The problem wasn’t one late email. It was a process repeatedly creating its own rework.

The Change I Implemented

Stop promoting every webinar as an isolated event.

I changed the promotional model.

Instead of building a completely separate initial promotional cycle for every webinar, Best Practice Webinars would receive one consolidated promotional email each month.

The rule was straightforward:

The New Boundary

  • One monthly promotional email
  • Only ready webinars were included
  • Late webinars no longer held up the others
  • Individual Tuesday reminders still continued

If your webinar information is ready by the deadline, it goes in the monthly email.

If it isn’t ready, the email still goes out.

The New Promotion Model

One Email. Multiple Webinars. More Time to Decide.

The monthly email presented the upcoming webinars together and directed people to the existing Best Practice Webinar landing page.

Once someone reached that page, they weren’t limited to the webinar that originally caught their attention.

They could see the other upcoming webinars and register for any that interested them.

That meant one promotional email could support several webinars at the same time.

Individual webinar reminders still went out on Tuesdays before each Wednesday event.

But those reminders also brought people back to the same central landing page, where the rest of the upcoming webinar schedule remained visible.

Instead of asking the audience to discover each webinar through a separate weekly promotional cycle, we gave them one place to see what was coming.

What This Improved

  • One email promoted multiple webinars
  • Audiences saw upcoming events earlier
  • People could register for multiple webinars at once
  • Reminder emails continued driving people to the same hub

Protecting the Schedule

What Happened If a Webinar Wasn’t Ready?

It didn’t stop the rest of the program.

If three webinars had everything needed for the monthly email and a fourth didn’t, the three ready webinars were promoted.

The fourth was not included simply because it had been planned.

Once its information became available, it could still be added to the webinar landing page.

And if legitimate space became available later in the email calendar, there could still be opportunities to promote it.

But its lateness no longer automatically meant that someone else’s properly scheduled campaign needed to move.

The Operating Rule

  • Ready work moved forward
  • Incomplete work did not block the month
  • Late webinars could still be added to the landing page later
  • Other teams kept their planned send slots
Urgency stopped being the default reward for being late.

What Changed

What the Redesign Improved

01

Less Repetitive Promotional Work

Multiple webinars could share one initial promotional communication instead of requiring separate initial campaigns every week.

02

More Time for Registration

Audiences could discover webinars earlier and register for multiple upcoming events.

03

Better Use of the Email Calendar

BPW consumed less of the limited sending capacity available to the shared audience.

04

More Predictable Execution

The monthly deadline created a clear point at which webinar information needed to be ready for promotion.

05

Fairer Prioritization

Teams that followed established processes were less likely to have properly planned work displaced by late BPW requests.

What the Redesign Revealed

The Process Worked — But It Revealed the Next Problem

For the first couple of months, the webinar information needed for the consolidated emails was provided and the model worked as intended.

But over time, the old behavior started to return.

Information wasn’t always ready by the monthly deadline.

I continued enforcing the process.

If the information wasn’t ready, the monthly email still went out.

That protected the downstream workflow.

But it also revealed the limit of what the redesign could accomplish.

What I Learned

  • The new boundary protected execution
  • The upstream planning behavior still remained
  • Process changes need management reinforcement to last
I could change what happened when late work reached me. I couldn’t make another team plan earlier.

The Next Recommendation

Replace year-ahead scheduling with quarterly planning.

The annual scheduling model was asking people to make commitments too far in advance.

I recommended shortening that planning horizon.

Instead of asking speakers near the end of the year to reliably commit to dates throughout the entire following year, webinar planning could happen one quarter at a time.

For example, in early February, begin planning April, May, and June.

Give prospective speakers a defined period to select their dates.

Once the quarter is established, begin gathering the information required for the earliest webinars first.

Rather than treating an April webinar and a June webinar as equally urgent simply because both exist, information could be collected according to when the work actually needs to begin.

The goal would be to have the required information available before downstream execution becomes urgent.

Quarterly Planning Model

  • Plan one quarter at a time
  • Get speaker commitments closer to the event
  • Start with the earliest webinars first
  • Gather information according to lead time
  • Reduce avoidable cancellations and late changes

Standardized Intake

Standardize How the Work Enters the System

Quarterly planning would address when webinars were planned.

The intake process also needed to address how the work was created.

Rather than duplicating an old Asana project dozens of times and leaving every downstream owner to manually correct their individual tasks, I recommended using a standardized request process.

An Asana intake form could collect the required webinar information and create the appropriate work from that information.

That would allow the process to begin with current information rather than an old project that everyone downstream had to correct.

Future-State Intake

  • Collect current information first
  • Create work from standardized intake
  • Use the correct task structure from the beginning
  • Reduce downstream cleanup

The Bigger Lesson

Creating work is not the same as making progress.

The original process was built with good intentions.

Creating the year’s webinar projects at once felt proactive.

It created visibility.

It put dates on a calendar.

It assigned responsibility.

But when I followed the process downstream, I saw the hidden cost.

Incomplete work had been distributed across multiple people months before some of it could be completed.

Those people then spent time maintaining the work, correcting dates, responding to changes, and accommodating urgency created by information that hadn’t been ready when the work was assigned.

The solution wasn’t to ask everyone downstream to manage their tasks faster.

It was to reconsider when the work should be created, what information should exist before it is created, and how much of the future we can realistically plan at once.

Systems Lesson

  • Visibility is not the same as readiness
  • Premature task creation can hide downstream cost
  • Boundaries protect capacity
  • Sustainable change needs accountability upstream
A process can create a boundary, but it can’t create accountability on behalf of another team.
